# Cut the checkout, prove reviews, rebuild the journal

The three pages need different things. The checkout needs fewer elements, not new styling. The review system needs honesty around a backend rule that is already sound. The blog and news pages need their content corrected before any layout work. The owner's dislike of the checkout is well founded. It spreads about one screen of decisions over three steps, repeats the order summary up to four times, and borrows Amazon yellow, Flipkart blue, mint, emoji and gradients that appear nowhere else on the site. Its price table also fails to add up: the test cart showed Subtotal ₹650, Discount −₹120, GST ₹33 and Total ₹683, when those rows sum to ₹563. The recommended replacement is a single page in the forest, gold and cream palette. It has two sections that collapse once complete (Delivery, then Payment), about seven visible fields, UPI listed first and COD last, one sticky summary whose rows add up, and one button that states the amount. Reviews already require a Delivered order containing the product, which is the core of what India's BIS standard IS 19000:2022 expects. Around that rule, though, the site still shows invented ratings ("4.7 (142)"), fake "Dr." testimonials with blood-sugar claims, and review text injected unescaped into the admin panel, which is a stored-XSS path to the admin's token. Some backend repairs are already in the uncommitted working tree. The news page is almost entirely unverifiable corporate PR: USDA certification, a ministry award, ISO 22000 and 200 farming families. The blog makes disease-treatment claims under unverified "Dr." bylines, and the FSSAI advertising rules prohibit that kind of claim. Both pages should merge into one "Journal" of recipes, grain guides and farm notes. Each article should be a static page with its own URL, a real byline and inline product cards. About a dozen decisions are business calls only the owner can make, listed at the end. They cover COD fee versus prepaid discount, GST-inclusive pricing, the login model, certifications and authorship.

**Scope and method.** This report is built from code audits of the working tree on 12 Nov 2026, browser checks at 1440 px and 375 px on a local server with the backend not running, and published research from Baymard, NN/g, Spiegel, Google and Indian regulators. Other sessions were editing the same files during the audit. The shared `js/site-shell.js` header and footer now replace the inline navigation on checkout, blog and news. Line numbers are therefore approximate: search for the function name before editing. Every Baymard figure comes from US or European shoppers, and no India-specific checkout usability benchmark was found.

## The checkout looks borrowed and its numbers don't add up

### What the audit found

The current `pages/checkoutmyorderpage.html` has three steps: "Cart & Address", "Order Summary" and "Payment". Step 2 shows only the items, the address and the price rows, and all of these already sit in the sticky sidebar. It adds a click and tells the shopper nothing new. The steps switch with `style.display` and never call `history.pushState`, so the phone's Back button leaves checkout entirely. The new-address form has seven inputs plus a Home/Work/Other picker. It already fills city and state from the PIN through api.postalpincode.in, which is good, but State is read-only, so a failed lookup blocks the order. Inputs render at 14 px on mobile, which triggers iOS zoom on focus. There is no email field, and Razorpay is prefilled with `email: ''`. The payment step includes a billing-address block with five fields, and its `selectedBilling` value is never sent in any order payload, so the block does nothing. Cart lines have no quantity control, because `cartQty()` exists but is never called. Each line instead has a pack-size dropdown that re-prices it on the client, so "2 × 0.5 kg" appears only as "₹180 / 0.5 kg" and the "2" is never shown. Every line says "Free Delivery" while the sidebar says delivery is free only above ₹999.

The arithmetic is the most damaging visual bug, because it sits exactly where money is shown. `calcTotal()` treats "Subtotal" as the selling price and shows "Discount" (MRP minus price) as a minus row, then adds GST on top, so the visible rows can never reconcile. Step 2 also leaves out the GST row while showing the same total. `deliveryCost` is hard-coded to 0. Meanwhile the server's `orderGuard.js` charges `SHIPPING_FEE` below `FREE_SHIPPING_ABOVE` (default ₹999) and rejects any order whose total differs by more than ₹1.5. Nothing breaks today only because `SHIPPING_FEE` defaults to 0. **The day the owner sets a delivery fee, every order under ₹999 will fail with "The order total does not match."** When a fee is non-zero, the same line writes the rupee sign with `textContent = '&#8377;' + deliveryCost`, which would print the literal entity text. Re-checked in the working copy on 12 Nov, all of these issues are still present.

The payment section is framed in developer language: "Payment Gateway: Choose your preferred payment provider". It shows a made-up blue Razorpay tile with a "Recommended" pill, then a row of hand-drawn text "logos" for UPI, VISA, RuPay, GPay and PhonePe on purple and orange gradients. Security claims ("256-bit SSL", "PCI-DSS Level 1", "RBI Compliant") appear at least six times. "Buyer Protected", "FSSAI Certified" and "100% Organic" tiles are drawn with emoji. "Terms & Conditions" and "Privacy Policy" are plain `<span>`s, even though `terms-and-conditions.html` and `privacy-policy.html` now exist. The Pay button is Amazon's `#FFD814`, the only yellow on the site, and "REMOVE" is Flipkart's `#2874f0`. COD shows a fake "Processing Payment… Secured by Razorpay" overlay that holds the shopper for a fixed 2.8 seconds before the order is even sent. The confirmation is a confetti modal with an invented "today + 5 days" delivery date and a bright WhatsApp-green button ahead of "View My Orders". The page loads four font families (Cormorant, Inter, Roboto, DM Mono) and contains 45 emoji entities. One of them, 🏷, rendered as an empty box in the test browser. With the new shared header, about 170 px of every desktop viewport is navigation chrome, including the full store nav and product search. At 375 px the order total first appears about 1,215 px down a 2,803 px page.

Two backend gaps shape what the redesign can promise. `POST /api/orders` (COD) requires login. `/razorpay/verify` saves the order only `if (orderPayload && req.user?.id)`, so a guest who paid online would be charged and have no order saved. The current front end avoids this only by forcing login at the Pay button. That is the worst moment to do it, and after login the guest's typed address is stored under a different localStorage key, so it is lost. The `User` model already has `phoneOtp` fields, but no OTP endpoints exist.

### What the research says a checkout should do

Field count and clarity matter more than step count. Baymard found that one-page checkouts are not automatically better, and that multi-step flows work when basic guidelines are followed ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/one-page-checkout)). In accordion-style checkouts, completed steps must collapse into a visible summary of what was entered, and Back must return to the previous step via `history.pushState()` ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/accordion-style-checkout)). The 2024 benchmark averaged **11.3 form fields against an ideal of about 8**. It also recommends a single "Full name" field, because 42% of test users typed their whole name into "First name". It recommends collapsing address line 2, defaulting billing to shipping, and hiding the coupon field behind a link ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/checkout-flow-average-form-fields)). An open coupon field sends some shoppers off-site to hunt for codes, and 83% of sites don't auto-apply eligible discounts. Trust comes mainly from a clear, predictable interface, and badges only "can help". The button should say exactly what happens next. Entered data must never be cleared on error, yet 34% of sites clear it ([Baymard Payment UX](https://baymard.com/learn/payment-ux)). Guest checkout should be a prominent button using the word "guest". 24% of US shoppers abandoned a cart in one quarter solely because they were forced to create an account ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/make-guest-checkout-prominent)). NN/g adds mobile specifics: put the order summary near the top, derive city and state from the postal code but keep them editable, use numeric keypads for phone and PIN, and list the most common payment method first ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-checkout-ux/)).

Indian context sharpens the payment ordering. UPI was **85.5% of payment transactions by volume** in H2 2025 ([RBI](https://rbi.org.in/scripts/PublicationsView.aspx?Id=23813)). Yet prepaid was only 41% of e-commerce orders in 2025 ([Social Samosa/ClickPost](https://www.socialsamosa.com/report/prepaid-e-commerce-orders-rise-10958917)), and D2C COD orders return to origin **37.51% of the time against 5.28% for prepaid** ([ClickPost](https://www.clickpost.ai/blog/ecommerce-rto-statistics-india)). The leading Indian organic brands outsource this whole flow. Two Brothers India Farms loads Shopflo, and Organic Tattva loads Shiprocket Checkout with GoKwik's phone-OTP layer, both observed in page scripts on 12 Nov 2026 ([Two Brothers](https://twobrothersindiashop.com/)). These products share one shape: mobile number first, OTP, a saved address card from a cross-merchant network, offers inside checkout, prepaid nudges, and COD gated by pincode, risk score, fee or OTP ([Shopflo](https://www.shopflo.com/); [GoKwik listing](https://apps.shopify.com/kwikcheckout)). Razorpay Magic Checkout offers similar prefill and COD-risk controls. Its docs list Shopify, WooCommerce and mobile SDKs, and they don't say whether a plain-HTML site with its own backend qualifies ([Razorpay docs](https://razorpay.com/docs/payments/magic-checkout/)). Razorpay's own case study of GirOrganic, an organic food brand, reports an 11% rise in conversions after prefill. That is a vendor figure ([Razorpay](https://razorpay.com/blog/magic-checkout-case-study-girorganic/)). Norms for COD fees (₹25–60 flat) and prepaid incentives (₹20–50 off) also come from vendor blogs. These sources themselves warn that blanket prepaid discounts mostly reward people who would have paid online anyway ([Razorpay](https://razorpay.com/blog/cash-on-delivery/); [Velocity](https://blog.velocity.in/cod-vs-prepaid-optimising-payment-options-to-reduce-rtos/)). Nansei can copy the shape of the flow in plain HTML. It cannot copy the cross-merchant address network without adopting one of these vendors. The live checkouts of Two Brothers and Organic Tattva were not opened, because that required entering a phone number, so their exact field order and COD fees are unverified.

### The recommended checkout

The new checkout is one page with two sections that open one at a time, Delivery and then Payment. It uses a `pushState` entry per section, so Android Back works. Desktop is a 1080 px container with a left column of up to 620 px and a 360 px sticky summary on the right. The background is cream `#f7f3ec`. Cards are white with a 1 px `#e6dfd2` border, a 12 px radius and no shadow. Headings use Cormorant Garamond 600, and everything else is Inter at 16 px. Roboto and DM Mono are dropped, and order numbers use Inter with `tabular-nums`. Gold `#c9a84c` appears only as thin accents: the active-section rule, focus rings and the free-delivery progress fill. It is never used as text on cream, where `#7a5f17` passes contrast. The single primary button is forest-deep `#0f2218` with cream text, 52 px tall, with no gradient and no glow. The page should use a reduced header: the logo links home, a "Secure checkout" line with a lock line-icon sits beside it, and the store nav and product search are hidden. Shoppers are then not pulled back into browsing. Progress is a text line, "Delivery · Payment", with a 2 px gold underline on the active item, not numbered circles.

The Delivery section shows saved addresses as radio cards for signed-in shoppers, with "+ Add a new address" as a link. New addresses use the order Baymard and NN/g support. First comes the mobile number (`type=tel`, `inputmode=numeric`, `autocomplete=tel-national`, "+91" shown), with the helper text "For delivery updates and the courier." Then come full name, and PIN (`inputmode=numeric`, `autocomplete=postal-code`), which fills in "Chennai, Tamil Nadu" inline. After that are house/flat, area or landmark (marked optional), city and State, both prefilled but editable, with State as a `<select>` of 36 states and UTs. Last is an optional email, "For your invoice and tracking link." Billing address is removed entirely. A collapsed "Need a GST invoice? Add GSTIN" link appears only if the owner wants B2B orders. `line1`, `line2`, `city`, `state` and `pincode` are stored as separate fields. Once complete, the section collapses to a one-line summary with "Change". Until guest checkout exists on the backend, the sign-in prompt belongs at the top of this section, with a `?next=` return. It does not belong at Pay.

The Payment section has three radio rows and no logo strip. "UPI (Google Pay, PhonePe, Paytm, any UPI app)" is preselected, with the helper text "You'll approve the payment in your UPI app." Next is "Card / Net banking / Wallet", and last is "Cash on delivery", which shows any COD fee and a phone or WhatsApp confirmation line if the owner adopts them. Both prepaid rows open the same Razorpay Standard Checkout. Passing a `config.display` preference so UPI shows first has not been verified and should be tested in Razorpay test mode before relying on it. The button label states the action: "Pay ₹683 securely" for prepaid, "Place order · Pay ₹683 on delivery" for COD. Under it sit exactly two muted lines: "Payments are processed by Razorpay. We never see or store your card or UPI PIN." and "By placing this order you agree to our Terms and Privacy Policy", with real links. Remove the PCI, RBI and "256-bit" strip, the "Amount Payable" box, the "Recommended" pill and the timed overlay. A spinner appears in the button only while a real request is running.

The summary is the visual anchor. It is titled "Your order (4 items)". Each row has a 56 px thumbnail, the name, the pack as "0.5 kg × 2", a −/+ stepper and "Remove" as a forest text link. The pack-size dropdown is removed, because pack choice belongs on the product page. A collapsed "Have a promo code?" link calls the existing `POST /api/coupons/validate` and sends `couponCode` with the order. Active automatic discounts from `GET /api/discounts/active` show as already applied. The price rows must sum on screen and mirror `orderGuard.js` exactly:

| Option A (MRP shown) | Option B (selling price shown) |
|---|---|
| Items (MRP) ₹770 | Items ₹650 |
| Discount on MRP −₹120 | GST 5% ₹33 |
| Delivery FREE / ₹X | Delivery FREE / ₹X |
| GST 5% ₹33 | **Total ₹683** |
| **Total ₹683** | "You save ₹120 on MRP" (small line) |

Under ₹999, a 4 px cream track with a gold fill reads "Add ₹349 more for free delivery". That threshold is lower than Two Brothers' ₹1,499 and worth showing. The trust area holds at most three plain, checkable lines: dispatch location and packing time, the 7-day damaged-or-wrong-item policy linked to `refund-policy.html`, and the FSSAI licence number once supplied. "Buyer Protected", "100% Organic" and PCI claims go unless a certificate backs them. On mobile there is a single summary: a collapsed cream bar at the top showing "Show order summary ▾ … ₹683", with the duplicate sidebar below it removed. A sticky bottom bar from section 1 onward shows "₹683 · 4 items" with the current CTA, respecting `env(safe-area-inset-bottom)`. Inputs are at least 48 px tall at 16 px text. Errors are inline and specific, for example "Enter a 10-digit mobile number starting with 6–9" and "We couldn't find this PIN — check it, or type city and state". A server 409 refreshes the cart and shows "Prices updated — please review your total". A dismissed Razorpay modal shows "Payment not completed. You can try again or choose cash on delivery", with nothing cleared.

My Orders should leave this file. It currently shares one 2,400-line script with checkout and has five modals, several of them broken for API orders. Move it to `pages/orders.html`, update the three `?page=orders` links in `site-shell.js`, and keep a one-line redirect from the old query. The confetti modal should become a calm confirmation state. It shows "Thank you, {first name}. Your order is placed." with the server's `orderNumber`, then either "Paid ₹683 by UPI · Payment ID pay_xxx" or "Pay ₹683 in cash when it arrives", then the items, the address, and "We pack orders within 1–2 working days; you'll get the tracking link by SMS/email". A delivery date is shown only once Shiprocket returns a real `etd`. The buttons are "Track this order", "Continue shopping" and a secondary text link to share on WhatsApp. Baymard recommends offering account creation here, after the order, rather than before checkout ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/make-guest-checkout-prominent)). Copy rules: no exclamation marks; no "seamless", "100% secure" or "Recommended"; the rupee sign via `&#8377;` in HTML and the literal "₹" character in JavaScript strings; and one brand spelling, "Nansei Organics".

## Reviews already require a delivered order; everything around that rule leaks

### Where the system stands

The backend core is sound. `POST /api/reviews` requires login, checks that the order belongs to the user, has `orderStatus === 'Delivered'` and contains the product, rejects duplicates through a unique `{user, product}` index, and then sets `isVerifiedPurchase: true`. `refreshProductRating()` recomputes the average and count from approved reviews after every write. That is a genuine server-side verified-purchase rule. Because it is the only way to create a review, every stored review is "verified". One loophole remains: an admin can mark any order Delivered, including one placed by staff, and so make its owner eligible. Nothing flags staff-written reviews.

Some of the weaknesses the audit found are already being fixed in uncommitted working-tree changes to `backend/routes/reviewRoutes.js` and `backend/models/Review.js`, checked on 12 Nov. Review text is now stripped of HTML tags on write. Public names are shown as first name plus last initial ("Priya S."). The public endpoint computes a per-star histogram from published reviews rather than trusting stored counts. The admin branch of `PUT /:id` is gone, so the store can no longer rewrite a customer's words. A new `PATCH /:id/visibility` hides a review only with a reason and records `hiddenReason` and `hiddenAt`. `PUT /:id/reply` stores a public store reply, `editedAt` is recorded, and `/my-reviews` now returns title and comment, which fixes the empty Edit modal. These changes should be committed. Several gaps remain. Admins can still hard-delete any review with no logged reason. There is no append-only moderation log. Public GETs have no pagination, sort or filter, don't validate the ObjectId, and still sit under the 100-requests-per-15-minutes write limiter. `/admin/all` returns everything in one response.

The front end is where most of the risk sits. `loadProductReviews` in `product.html` still concatenates `r.comment` into `innerHTML` (`'<p class="rev-text">' + r.comment + '</p>'`). The admin panel still injects `r.title` raw (`admin-panel.html` ~2008) on a page that keeps its JWT in `localStorage`. Server-side stripping protects new reviews only. Any record written before the change still carries whatever HTML was submitted, so **client-side escaping remains a P0 security fix**. `renderStars()` draws a full star whenever `i − r < 1`, so a 4.1 rating shows as five full stars. A hard-coded "Verified" label shows under the score even when there are zero reviews. Two empty states fight each other through a monkey-patched `innerHTML`. The distribution bars show a bare percentage, aren't clickable, and draw on a different data source from the headline count. A dead `localStorage['rev_<pid>_<user>']` reader can still prepend a self-made "Verified Purchase" card and add 1 to the visible count without contacting the server.

Fake numbers persist in four places. First, `seed.js` and `seed-products.js` insert values like `rating: 4.7, numReviews: 142` into MongoDB, and those stay until a product's first real review triggers a recompute. Second, the inline catalogues on the home page, wishlist (which never calls the API) and account page render "4.7 (142)". Third, the product page's "You May Also Like", "New" and "Bestsellers" rows render from the static array even when the API works. Fourth, the product-page catalogue carries invented `verified: true` reviews for all 16 products. Some put medical claims in the mouths of fake professionals, such as a "Dr. Kamala N." who recommends a rice "to my patients for blood sugar management". The unused `NS_REVIEWS` array includes "Blood sugar improved from 210 to 148 after 3 months". If the API product load fails, a Mongo id falls back to Karunguruvai Rice with 142 reviews. The live database was not inspected, so it is unknown whether production products still carry seeded counts.

### What shoppers and regulators expect

The research supports honest, sparse displays over impressive ones. 95% of Baymard's test users used reviews, and the **ratings distribution was the most-used part, relied on more than review text**. 90% of users who wanted a specific rating tried clicking the bars, yet 39% of sites don't make them clickable. Only 7% filtered when the filter was hidden by default, and 53% actively sought negative reviews ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/user-ratings-distribution-summary)). Baymard recommends hiding the distribution at five or fewer ratings, since users preferred 4.5 stars from 12 ratings over 5 stars from 2. About twice as many respondents chose 4.5 stars from 57 ratings over 5 stars from 4, so cards should always pair stars with a count ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/user-perception-of-product-ratings)). Spiegel Research Center found that **five reviews raise purchase likelihood by 270% over zero**, with diminishing returns after that. Purchase likelihood peaks at 4.0–4.7 stars and falls toward 5.0 as ratings look "too good to be true" ([Spiegel](https://spiegel.medill.northwestern.edu/How-Online-Reviews-Influence-Sales)). The seeded 4.7–4.8 scores, combined with a star renderer that rounds up, push every product toward exactly that suspicious look. Store replies matter: 87% of sites don't respond to negative reviews, and 37% of users factor the response into their judgment ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/respond-to-negative-user-reviews)).

On collection, prompted reviews skew up to about half a star higher than self-motivated ones, and prompting does not reduce organic volume ([Spiegel](https://spiegel.medill.northwestern.edu/self-motivated-vs-prompted-reviews)). That makes even-handed moderation more important once the store starts asking. PowerReviews, a vendor source, recommends 14 days after purchase for perishables and a single follow-up 7 days later. It reports that 68% of reviewers respond to the first request and 77% review within a week of using the product ([PowerReviews](https://www.powerreviews.com/when-to-ask-for-reviews-best-practice-guide/)). Rice and dried flowers get cooked over several days. Anchoring the first email **7–10 days after `deliveredAt`** with one reminder fits both patterns. That timing is an inference, not a tested figure for Indian shoppers. On WhatsApp, Meta treats a review request as a cheaper "utility" message only if it refers to a specific order. Mixing in a discount turns it into "marketing" ([Meta](https://developers.facebook.com/documentation/business-messaging/whatsapp/templates/template-categorization)).

Indian rules make the fake ratings a compliance problem as well as a trust problem. The Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules 2020 bar a seller from falsely representing itself as a consumer to post reviews ([LiveLaw](https://www.livelaw.in/columns/consumer-protection-e-commerce-rules-2020-a-step-in-the-right-direction-160858)). Inventory sellers like Nansei must not post fake reviews or misrepresent product quality ([IndiaLaw](https://www.indialaw.in/blog/civil/consumer-protection-e-commerce-rules/)). BIS standard IS 19000:2022 took effect on 25 November 2022 and covers any organisation publishing consumer reviews, including sellers collecting from their own customers ([PIB](https://pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=1877733); [Vikaspedia](https://vikaspedia.in/social-welfare/social-awareness/consumer-education/bis-framework-for-online-consumer-reviews)). Its provisions include verifying authors, moderating to filter bias and fraud, and showing the publishing date and star rating ([The Statesman](https://www.thestatesman.com/business/centre-launches-framework-on-fake-and-deceptive-reviews-in-e-commerce-1503133479.html/amp)). Legal summaries add equal moderation regardless of sentiment, publication without undue delay (about 72 hours), a business right to respond, and separate marking of incentivised reviews, which must not influence the overall rating ([S.S. Rana & Co.](https://ssrana.in/articles/bis-standards-for-verifying-consumer-reviews-ensuring-transparency-integrity/)). The standard is voluntary for now. The Centre has said it plans to make it mandatory ([Outlook Business](https://www.outlookbusiness.com/news/centre-to-make-framework-against-fake-reviews-mandatory-for-ecommerce-platforms)), and a draft Quality Control Order was discussed in 2024 ([India Today NE](https://www.indiatodayne.in/national/story/consumer-affairs-crackdown-on-fake-online-reviews-quality-control-order-issued-1007122-2024-05-15)). Once mandatory, violations would be treated as unfair trade practices ([Legal500](https://www.legal500.com/intelligence/india/consumer-protection/framework-launched-by-the-government-of-india-to-restrict-fake-reviews-on-e-commerce-sites)). The CCPA's 2023 dark-patterns guidelines also cover ads disguised as user content ([Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/india/consumer-trading-unfair-trading/1398262/ccpa-issues-guidelines-for-prevention-and-regulation-of-dark-patterns-2023-effective-30-november-2023)). Neither the standard's full text nor the gazette was readable, so these clause-level points rest on secondary summaries. Whether a Quality Control Order has been notified as of November 2026 is unconfirmed.

### The recommended review system

For the data, finish what the working tree started. Add `isStaff`, set when the author is an admin or on a staff list and displayed as "Nansei team" instead of the verified chip. Add `isIncentivised`, excluded from the average. Add `variantLabel`, copied from the order item so cards can read "Verified purchase · 1 kg". Add an append-only `moderation: [{action, reason, by, at}]` log. Store `ratingHistogram` on Product, and drop the unbounded `reviews: [ObjectId]` array that `getProduct` populates in full. A one-off script should reset every product's `rating` and `numReviews` from a real aggregate (zero where none exist), and the seed files should stop writing them. Endpoints should become `GET /api/reviews/product/:id?sort=recent|helpful|highest|lowest&rating=&page=&limit=10`, with ObjectId validation and the public rate limiter. Add `POST /:id/helpful` with one vote per user, and a paginated admin list filterable by status, rating and product. Admin hard delete should survive only for legal removal requests, with a logged reason. Otherwise the store hides a review. Client rendering must use `textContent` or an `esc()` helper everywhere. The same helper already exists in `backend/utils/shippingEmails.js`.

The admin Reviews page gets Published and Hidden tabs plus a "Needs reply" filter for reviews of three stars or fewer with no reply. Each row shows the masked customer, stars, escaped text, order number and delivery date. The only actions are Reply, Hide (a picklist of abusive language, personal information, not about the product, spam or duplicate, legal request) and Restore. There is no Edit button. Publish a short review policy, for example as a section in `terms-and-conditions.html`. It should name exactly those removal reasons, say that negative reviews are published, and say that the store may reply.

The product page's existing `#reviewsSection` keeps its eyebrow and serif heading. The summary card shows the score in forest-deep with true half-stars in gold, and the line "Based on 12 reviews from verified buyers". The static "Verified" label is deleted. Five count bars ("5 ★ ▬▬ 9") in forest-leaf `#4a8c5c` on a pale-green track act as single-select filter buttons with `aria-pressed`, and they are hidden at five or fewer reviews. A toolbar offers sorting by Most recent (the default; that choice is a recommendation, not a cited rule), Most helpful, Highest and Lowest, plus an active-filter pill. Each review card shows "Priya S.", the date, the stars, a "Verified purchase · 1 kg" chip, a bold title, the comment and "Helpful (3)". A store reply appears as an indented cream block with a 3 px gold left border, labelled "Reply from Nansei Organics". There are no gradient avatars and no "Featured" cards. "Show more reviews" pages from the server. With zero reviews there are no stars and no "New" pill, only one calm line: "No reviews yet. Reviews come from customers whose order was delivered." An eligible buyer also sees an inline "Write the first review" button. The rating row near the price reads "★ 4.6 · 12 reviews" and links to the section, and it disappears entirely at zero. Product cards on the home page, related rows, wishlist and account use only API data: `numReviews > 0 ? "★ 4.6 · 12" : ''`. On API failure they show nothing rather than static numbers. `NS_REVIEWS`, `nsRenderReviews`, every inline `rating`/`reviews`/`reviewData` and the `rev_*` localStorage reader are deleted.

Collection starts on the delivered order row in My Orders. Five outline stars sit there, and tapping one opens the modal prefilled with that rating. The modal has the same fields as the product-page form, which today lacks a title field. Under the submit button, one line reads "Your review is published with your first name and last initial. We only remove reviews that break our review policy." A daily job finds orders delivered 7–10 days earlier with an unreviewed item and no `reviewRequestSentAt`. It sends one email styled like the existing delivered email (forest header, gold button "Rate your Karunguruvai Rice") with star links that deep-link into that modal. It sends one reminder 7 days later and then stops. It asks every delivered customer, never only the happy ones. No coupon is offered for reviews. WhatsApp requests go only to opted-in customers, through an order-specific utility template with no promotional content.

## The news page is invented, and the blog can't be found or trusted

### What is on the pages now

Both pages are hard-coded single-URL JavaScript apps. The blog holds 18 articles in a `BLOGS` array, and the news page holds 8 items in `NEWS`, all dated January to April 2026, which is 7–10 months stale. Every article opens in a modal that changes neither the URL nor `document.title`, so no article can be found in search, shared or bookmarked. Google states that it can discover links only if they are `<a>` elements with an `href`, and it advises distinct URLs for distinct content ([Google](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/javascript/javascript-seo-basics)). The blog's cards are `<article onclick>` with an `href`-less "Read" link. The admin panel publishes to `/api/blogs` and `/api/news`, but blog.html never fetches the API. news.html fetches `window.API_BASE || 'http://localhost:5000/api'` without loading `js/config.js`, so production visitors call localhost, which was still true on 12 Nov. Even a working API would fail here, because Mongo ids are interpolated unquoted into `onclick`. **Content the owner publishes from the admin panel therefore never reaches shoppers.** The news newsletter form shows "Subscribed!" and sends nothing.

The blog's 3D carousel auto-advances every 1.8 seconds with no pause control. That fails WCAG 2.2.2, which requires pause, stop or hide for auto-moving content running more than 5 seconds ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/pause-stop-hide.html)). It also fills the first mobile screen, pushing the first article to about 785 px, behind the bottom bar. Neither page has an H1. With the shared header now in place, the blog has also lost its article search, because the shell's search box searches products. The modal shows literal "[[ICON:WHEAT]] RICE VARIETIES" and "[[icon:shopping-bag]]" text. Reading comfort is poor: body text is 14 px at about 89 characters per line, meta text is 10 px and chips are 8 px. Baymard recommends 50–75 characters, about `max-width: 70ch`, and found users fatigued on lines of 100 or more characters ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/line-length-readability)). 16 px is the common body-text floor ([Smashing Magazine](https://smashingmagazine.com/2011/10/16-pixels-body-copy-anything-less-costly-mistake/)). Hero images are pack shots with baked-in label text. Two articles use the wrong product's photo, six use Unsplash stock, and category chips use pink, cyan, blue, orange and lime, none of which are in the brand palette. The modal does link to the correct `product.html?id=N`, but only as a single text button, with no price, pack size or add-to-cart.

The content problem outweighs the layout problem. The news page claims USDA Organic certification after "148 inspection criteria", nine certifications including Halal and Kosher, a "Best Organic Brand" award "presented by the Ministry of Agriculture", ISO 22000 renewed three years running at a "Madurai processing facility", 200 farming families "earning 40% more", a downloadable soil report that doesn't exist (whose numbers don't add up), a school programme and an auto-monthly subscription that doesn't exist. It lists a ₹105 price where the product page says ₹210, and pack sizes that don't match the product variants. The blog repeats "25-acre certified farm", "since 2003", "60% solar" and "seven rice varieties" claims that conflict with other pages. It places varieties in the "Cauvery delta" when the farm is at Achankulam near Srivilliputhur. The product pages list FSSAI only. The health claims are the legal exposure. Karunguruvai is "an excellent daily staple for people managing Type 2 diabetes". Poongar is "safe for women who develop gestational diabetes". Hibiscus is "comparable to some pharmaceutical interventions". Country fig is "a genuinely effective natural remedy for iron-deficiency anemia". The bylines are "Dr. Nithya Kumar" four times, "Dr. Priya Selvam", two chefs and a nutritionist, none with a bio or credential. The FSSAI (Advertising and Claims) Regulations 2018 prohibit claims that a food prevents, treats or cures a disease, except where specifically permitted. Reduction-of-risk wording is the allowed form ([FSSAI digest](https://fssai-digest.fssai.gov.in/research-and-innovations/claims.php); [AM Legals](https://amlegals.com/food-safety-and-standards-advertising-and-claims-regulations-2018-a-deeper-dive-into-compliance/)). Google likewise names "fabricating creator profiles" as a negative quality signal and holds health content to expert consensus ([Google](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content)). Two details need an expert check: whether the hibiscus sold is *H. sabdariffa* or the *H. rosa-sinensis* usually called "Sembaruthi", and whether website articles count as "advertisement" under the gazette wording. Neither the gazette text nor the species was verified.

### Merge both into one journal of recipes and farm notes

None of the comparable brands keeps a lively separate newsroom. Organic Tattva's `/blogs/news` page shows one undated item. Its real content is a single dated blog of festival-timed food posts, each with its own URL ([Organic Tattva news](https://organictattva.com/blogs/news); [Organic Tattva blog](https://organictattva.com/blogs/blogs)). Pro Nature keeps one blog of practical comparisons, though its template leftovers and "admin" bylines show how neglect reads ([Pro Nature](https://www.pronatureorganic.com/blog)). Two Brothers runs "Farmers Kitaab" with recipe tags such as South Indian lunch ([TBOF](https://twobrothersindiashop.com/en-us/blogs/farmers-kitaab/tagged/south-india-lunch)). The CIKS heritage-rice programme Namma Nellu organises recipes by rice variety, each at its own URL ([Namma Nellu](https://nammanellu.com/recipes)). That model suits a store whose customers buy by variety. The recommendation is one nav item, **Journal**, with three types: Recipes, Know your grain, and From the farm. Real updates such as a new harvest or a new pack size become dated "From the farm" posts. `news.html` gets a 301 to the Journal in `netlify.toml`, and a "Featured in" strip appears on the About page only when real press exists.

Each article needs its own static page, `pages/journal/<slug>.html`, with a real `<title>`, meta description, canonical and Open Graph image. It also needs `BlogPosting` JSON-LD with `headline`, `image`, `datePublished`, `dateModified` and an `author` given as a real Person or the farm as an Organization, without honorifics in `author.name` ([Google Article](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/article)). Recipe pages add `Recipe` markup with ingredients, steps, yield and times, and Google requires a unique URL per recipe plus a summary page ([Google Recipe](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/recipe)). There are three ways to build this:

| Option | How it works | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| A. Static pages from a data file (recommended) | A small Node script reads `content/journal/*.json` and writes each article page and the listing with real `<a href>` cards and JSON-LD in the source | Best for search and speed; publishing needs a developer or a script run |
| B. API-driven template | `article.html?slug=` fetches a new `GET /api/blogs/slug/:slug`; the Blog model gains `slug`, `type`, `productIds[]`, `publishedAt`, `description`, `heroAlt`, `author{name,role,url}` and optional recipe fields | Owner publishes from the admin panel; content is rendered by JS and depends on the Render backend being awake |
| A+B hybrid | The admin panel stays the editor; a Netlify build step pulls `/api/blogs` and writes static pages | Both benefits, at the cost of a build step |

The listing page drops the carousel. On cream, it shows a small-caps gold label "JOURNAL", an H1 in Cormorant ("Cooking and growing heritage rice"), and one plain sentence naming Achankulam. Below that come text tabs (All · Recipes · Know your grain · From the farm) with a 2 px gold underline on the active tab, an optional variety `<select>`, and a small in-page article search. Then one lead story with a 16:9 image, and a three-column grid. Each card is a single `<a href>` with a 4:3 photo, a small-caps type label in gold-dark `#b8922a` (no coloured chip), a 22 px Cormorant title, a two-line excerpt and the date. The listing ends with a row of the existing, unchanged product cards for the featured varieties. On mobile, compact rows (a 96 px thumbnail with title and date) after the lead story fit four or five stories per screen, and nothing moves on its own. The article page uses a breadcrumb, an H1 of 40–44 px (30 px mobile), a 20 px standfirst, and a byline of 13–14 px such as "By Nansei farm kitchen · 12 Mar 2026 · 6 min read". It has a captioned hero photo of the dish or field, not the pack, and an 18 px Inter body (17 px mobile) at line-height 1.65 within `max-width: 66ch`, using real `<h2>`s. Recipes get a recipe box after the intro: a 1 px forest border with no shadow, yield, soak, prep and cook times, rice-to-water ratio and cooker whistles, ingredients with the store's own grain linked, numbered steps and a Print link. A product card with price, pack size and Add appears right after the ingredients and again in a "Cooked with" row at the end, followed by "More recipes using Karuppu Kavuni". Images use ImageKit `?tr=w-800` with width and height attributes. The current 3375 px PNGs shown at 431 px are the problem this solves. What keeps these pages from looking machine-made is mostly restraint: hairline rules, small-caps labels, dated captions naming place and month, and the farm's own gallery photos and video. Avoid gradient overlays, glow backgrounds, rotating carousels, emoji, "PINNED" badges, tickers, stagger fade-ins and words like "superfood" or "powerhouse".

For content, about eight to ten honest pieces can be rewritten from the current 18. Karuppu Kavuni becomes "How to cook Karuppu Kavuni, and a payasam", without the superfood and cholesterol lines. Vellai Kavuni becomes a Pongal-season sweet pongal recipe. Mappillai Samba keeps its bridegroom story as tradition and drops the testosterone claims. Karunguruvai and Poongar become kanji and cooking guides, with every diabetes, GI and anaemia claim removed. Rose milk and hibiscus sherbet become drink recipes, with the duplicate rose-milk recipe merged. Hibiscus, Avarampoo and Butterfly Pea become brewing guides with one dose matching the product page. Idiyappam, the health mix and fig powder become "ways to use" recipes with their real product images. Traditional use is framed as tradition ("in many Tamil homes, Poongar kanji is given after childbirth"), never as efficacy. The "Zero Chemicals" piece and all eight news items are cut until documents exist. One honest "Our farm at Achankulam" piece, written from what the owner can document, replaces them. One real post a month, timed to harvests and festivals, matches what Organic Tattva does.

## Conclusion

Across all three pages, the honest version is also the calmer and more distinctive one. A checkout with two sections, three payment rows and a summary that adds up has fewer elements than the current page. A review section that shows nothing until a real customer writes something looks emptier than "4.7 (142)" but holds up when checked. A journal built from eight cooking guides with real dates and the farm's name has fewer pages than eighteen articles plus a newsroom, but every page can be found, shared and believed. The owner's sense that something is wrong with these pages points mostly at borrowed colours, repeated badges, invented numbers and claims, and a carousel that never stops. Removing them is most of the redesign.

The second takeaway is about which risks are urgent. The most consequential problems are not visual. A delivery-fee setting would silently break every small order. A review comment can run script in the admin's session. Fake doctors on the blog promise diabetes control under rules that forbid it. None of these show in a screenshot, and all of them can be fixed before any visual redesign the owner must approve. The structural lesson from the earlier audit applies again here: when the catalogue, the ratings and the content live in three hand-copied places per page, fabricated numbers come back after every restore. Making the API the only source of ratings, and a data file the only source of articles, prevents that.
